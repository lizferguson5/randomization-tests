Shuffle the Labels: A Randomization Test Lab

If the treatment really didn't matter, the group labels would be meaningless. Shuffle them yourself, thousands of times, and see how often chance alone produces a difference as big as the real one.

STEP 1Meet the data

Pseudoscorpion mating experiment

Female pseudoscorpions were mated either with the same male twice (SM) or with two different males (DM). Researchers counted how many broods (egg clutches) each female produced.

Question: does mating with two different males change reproductive success?

Null hypothesis H0: μSM = μDM (mating treatment has no effect on mean broods)
Alternative HA: μSM ≠ μDM

Why not a t-test? The data are counts with lots of zeros, they aren't normal, and the distributions have different shapes. A randomization test needs no assumption about the shape of the data.

What would the data look like if the labels didn't matter?

  • Every female keeps her real number of broods; only her group label is reassigned at random.
  • The group sizes stay the same: 77 in SM and 72 in DM.
  • Shuffling is without replacement: every value is used exactly once.

Add some shuffles to calculate a p-value.
Why two tails? HA says the means are different, in either direction. A shuffle of −0.90 and one of +0.90 are equally surprising under H0, so both tails count.
Why the +1? The real arrangement of labels is itself one of the arrangements that could happen under H0, so it is added to both the count and the total.
